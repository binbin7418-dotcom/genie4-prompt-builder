1. Game prototyping and level mood tests

The job. A level designer wants to know whether a harbor town feels right at dusk before anyone grey-boxes it. Instead of a week of blockout, she writes the world, walks it, and steers the weather live to test both moods in one session.

Example prompt. “World: a cliffside fishing village above a harbor, blue-hour dusk. Weather: rolling fog banks. Camera: slow forward walk along the waterline. Audio: waves, gulls and a distant bell, no music.” Steering message at ~20s: “fog rolls in and the far background disappears.”

Why Genie 4. The turning point is the test: if the world stays readable and atmospheric when the fog arrives, the level concept survives contact with reality — in an afternoon, not a milestone.

2. Training simulations that fight back

The job. A safety instructor runs evacuation drills. Static simulators repeat; trainees memorize them. With steering, the instructor changes the drill while it runs — a blocked exit, rising water, a crowd forming — and every run is different.

Example prompt. “World: a medieval market square on fair day, bright midday. Weather: calm after a storm. Camera: slow forward walk through the scene. Audio: natural ambience of the place, no music.” Steering message at ~30s: “festival lanterns switch on one street at a time” — or, for the drill version, smoke, alarms and a shifting crowd.

Why Genie 4. New scenarios cost a paragraph. The instructor's library of drills grows as fast as she can write turning events.

3. Film previsualization

The job. A director needs to scout a location that doesn't exist — an orbital station garden — and rehearse the exact beat where the scene turns, before the production spends anything on sets or renders.

Example prompt. “World: an orbital station garden ring, sunrise alpenglow through the canopy glass. Weather: humid stillness. Camera: gentle aerial rise above the planting beds. Audio: deep quiet with one far-off sound source.” Steering message at ~25s: “a large shadow passes over the scene from above” — the eclipse beat, rehearsed.

Why Genie 4. Camera path is a prompt ingredient, so shot design and location design happen in the same pass, with the audio bed as a first sound sketch.

4. Classroom worlds for history and geography

The job. A teacher wants students inside the place the textbook describes — not a photo of it. The class walks a living scene, and the teacher steers events while narrating: the tide turning, the market waking up.

Example prompt. “World: an iceberg lagoon at the glacier face, bright midday. Weather: steady breeze, clear sky. Camera: side-tracking shot along the glacier face. Audio: wind, cracking ice and layered birdsong.” Steering message at ~20s: “the tide turns and water flows the other way.”

Why Genie 4. The lesson is the change, not the postcard: students watch a process happen instead of reading that it happens.

5. Virtual tourism and real-estate walkthroughs

The job. A travel creator previews destinations and story angles before booking flights; an agent walks buyers through a renovation concept that is still a floor plan. Both need places that feel inhabited, not rendered.

Example prompt. “World: a bookshop with a sleeping cat, golden hour, long shadows. Weather: light drizzle starting outside the windows. Camera: a push-in toward the most interesting doorway. Audio: rain on canvas and wooden surfaces.” Steering message at ~15s: “a door slowly opens, spilling warm light.”

Why Genie 4. Weather and light are ingredients, so the same place can be previewed at dawn, dusk and in the rain — the three versions a buyer or viewer actually decides between.

FAQ

Can Genie 4 be used for game development?

For prototyping and level mood-testing, yes: a designer can walk a described environment in minutes instead of grey-boxing it for days. It is a world model, not a game engine — there is no exportable geometry or game logic yet, so treat it as the fastest sketchbook a level designer has ever had.

Is Genie 4 useful for training simulations?

Yes, because of steering. An instructor can change conditions mid-run — weather closing in, a crowd gathering, equipment appearing — and the trainee reacts to a world that reacts back. Scenarios are written as prompts, so a new drill costs a paragraph, not a development sprint.

Can filmmakers use Genie 4 for previsualization?

Yes. A director can scout a location that does not exist yet, test camera paths through it, and rehearse the moment the scene changes — the turning event — before any set is built. The audio bed in the prompt doubles as a first sound-design sketch.
